Section 1 of 2·Persuasion

1.Persuasion: simple techniques

Almost every message that tries to persuade makes the same mistake: it talks about the person who 1writes. It talks about the product, the company, the seller’s competence. True persuasion does the opposite—it starts with the other. People respond emotionally to messages that touch their desires, fears, and beliefs; when you write from that place, you stop pushing and start being heard.

The simplest structure that captures this fits into five triggers. Encourage dreams of the person — support what they already want for themselves (“you were born for this”). Justify failures — ease the guilt by pointing to an external cause (“you didn’t have the right opportunities”). Reduce the fears — acknowledge the concern and offer a path (“it’s normal to feel this way at first, and here’s what to do”). Confirming suspicions — validate what they already suspect (“you were right to doubt that”). And help take on the enemies — build a connection by identifying a common adversary.2

The test that changes everything: who are you looking at?

Notice that none of the five describes the author. They all describe the reader’s internal state. That’s the point: most messages focus on the speaker, but true persuasion happens when you focus on the other person’s emotions and needs. Once you see the structure, you start recognizing it in all kinds of content that works — from the sales email to the political speech, from a viral post to a convincing conversation.

Stop and predict

You’re responding to someone who says: “I’ve tried selling online, but nothing works.” Which of the five triggers would you use first — and why?

See a possible answer

Probably justify failure: lifting the weight of guilt (“you were missing the right knowledge; it wasn't your fault”) makes the person more open to hearing the rest. Only then do encourage the dream e reduce fear. The order matters: relief before promise.

▸ Going deeper: turning the five triggers into a customer service prompt optional

The five triggers aren’t just for writing by hand. They can be built into a master prompt that guides an intelligent assistant to read the user’s message, identify its emotional content (dreams, fears, frustrations, beliefs, perceived enemies), and respond using at least three of the triggers—always ending with a practical next step.

# Master Prompt — Persuasive Intelligent Support Act as a precise, empathetic, and persuasive customer service assistant. When you receive the user's message: 1. Analyze the emotional and rational content (dreams, fears, frustrations, beliefs, enemies). 2. Look for data, examples, or clear instructions that meet the real need. 3. Use at least 3 of these triggers: encourage dreams, justify failures, reduce fears, confirm suspicions, help attack enemies. 4. Write in natural, welcoming, and confident language. 5. Finish with practical guidance or a clear next step.

The same template can be adapted for WhatsApp flows, chatbots, or human support that uses AI as a copilot — what changes is the channel, not the emotional structure.

Section 2 of 2·Viral story

2.The Anatomy of a Viral Story (VIRAL ST)

A story that sticks isn’t a matter of luck. It follows, in order, a circuit the brain recognizes — and each stage activates a different region. The VIRALST model organizes this circuit into four beats: hook, identification, conflict, and resolution. Each one answers a question the previous one raised, and that chain holds attention until the end.

The identification activates mirror neurons: natural language and everyday situations make people feel “this is about me.” The conflict creates tension — a little dissonance, an emotional contrast — that sustains interest through the expectation of resolution. It is the closing delivers the turning point: the insight that makes the brain release dopamine and oxytocin, etching the content into memory.3

The deliberately completed reward cycle

The four beats aren't independent: they form a dopamine loop. Curiosity at the opening, empathy in the identification, tension in the conflict, cognitive reward at the end—the same mechanism that makes TV series and short videos addictive, used here intentionally. Pacing matters as much as content: six to ten short lines, brief sentences, without logical connectors. The text is read like spoken words, not prose—and that’s what keeps the emotional flow going, not the rational one.

Stop and predict

Of the four beats, which one do scriptwriters tend to neglect the most—and what is the cost of skipping it?

See a possible answer

A identification. Many people go straight from the hook to the content, and the viewer never feels that “it's about them.” Without this emotional mirror, there's no connection—and the brain, which decides whether to keep watching at exactly this stage, simply scrolls to the next video.

▸ Going deeper: why an oral rhythm helps with retention optional

Optional layer for those who want the mechanics behind it. Short sentences reduce cognitive load — the left temporal cortex processes each line effortlessly. The absence of formal connectives imitates the natural rhythm of speech, maintaining synchrony between hearing and emotion. And the pauses (the line breaks) mimic the breathing of human speech, reinforcing the perception of authenticity.

There’s also an attention limit at play: the brain processes stimuli in short windows, and six to ten lines respect that interval. Going beyond that doesn’t make the text more informative — it makes it more tiring, and the viewer gives up before the ending, which was exactly where the reward was.
