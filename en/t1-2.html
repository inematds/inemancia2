The economics of distraction

Section 1 of 3·Shiny object

1.Shiny Object Syndrome (SOS)

If the previous section was about consumed distraction, this one is about distraction produced. A Shiny Object Syndrome and is the tendency to abandon what’s in progress whenever a more exciting new idea, tool, or project comes along. Every new thing shines—and focus slips toward it, leaving behind what was already close to bearing fruit.4

The cost is concrete. Switching projects before finishing wastes resources—time, money, team—without any real return. Abandoning an idea that would have worked if completed costs you opportunities. And there’s the invisible cost of the time it takes to ramp-up: every beginning requires a period of time to reach efficiency, and those who jump from one thing to another never reap the benefits of that learning. Many projects begin; almost none are finished.

The discipline of filtering ideas

The defense isn't about stopping yourself from having ideas—it's about filtering them. It starts with clarity of goals: the clearer the destination, the easier it is to see when a new opportunity pulls you in the wrong direction. Then comes the filter question to ask yourself when faced with each temptation: does this contribute significantly to my main goal? If it doesn’t solve a real problem or align with your goals, maybe it isn’t the right time. That’s the golden rule: start something new only when you’ve finished or systematized what was already underway.

Stop and predict

Slow progress isn’t the same as a bad project. Before jumping to the next brilliant idea, what would you look at to decide between continue or give up?

See a possible answer

Trends, not just the number for the day. Small but steady growth, rising retention, improving feedback — signs of sustainable progress call for patience. Prolonged stagnation, customer acquisition costs always exceeding customer value, or a market trend turning against you are signs that it may be time to shift your focus — based on data, not boredom.

Section 2 of 3·Efficiency

2.Efficiency x Distraction

Some people treat focus like a high-performance sport. The investor Kevin O'Leary, from the show Shark Tank, describes Elon Musk’s approach this way: if you’re not saying something valuable, he simply walks away—in the middle of the conversation, without apologizing. To O'Leary, Musk and Steve Jobs operated on the same wavelength, where distractions are eliminated without mercy.5

The metaphor he uses is simple. Signal is the time spent making progress toward your goals; noise and everything else—distractions, useless meetings, small talk. Spending too much time on the latter, O’Leary says, is a failure. Both Jobs and Musk are said to have mastered this balance. It’s not personal; it’s a matter of efficiency.

Signal over noise, in numbers

O'Leary, who worked with Jobs at Apple, recalls meetings where he was brutally focused—to the point of immediately rejecting a proposal to spend millions on market research, saying that “nobody knows what they want until I tell them.” In O'Leary’s estimation, Jobs was about “85% signal, 15% noise”: he still let some distractions through. Musk would be the rare case of “100% signal,” the most focused man he’d ever met. The attitude may sound arrogant, but the argument is that it was this—ignoring distractions and making decisions with confidence—that helped build companies that changed the world.

▸ Going deeper: what to keep and what to take with a grain of salt optional

Optional layer. The story is inspiring, but it’s worth reading carefully.

The useful core and the distinction signal vs. noise: it is indeed worth asking, for each task, whether it advances a goal or just consumes time. That's the thread connecting this section to Shiny Object Syndrome — both are about protecting what matters from what merely seems urgent. On the other hand, O'Leary's account describes a personal style, not a universal recipe: treating people harshly isn't what makes focus effective, and the numbers ("85%", "100%") are a figure of speech, not a measurement. Keep the principle — less noise, more signal — and discard the rudeness.

Section 3 of 3·Vulnerability

3.2025: The Main Vulnerability

Here the learning path comes full circle. In 2025, the argument goes, the main vulnerability of organizations ceases to be technique and becomes human. Even with reinforced firewalls, encryption, and infrastructure, the weakest link remains people's behavior—the so-called human algorithm.6

The consequence is a shift in focus away from defense. From perimeter of networks and packages for the cognitive defense: monitoring the misuse of trust, identifying subtle manipulation in messages — social engineering, sophisticated phishing, decision overload. From simple detection for the predicting human patterns: anticipating, for example, when someone is experiencing digital fatigue and is more likely to click a suspicious link.

From a network firewall to a firewall in the mind

That’s why security stops being just technical and becomes educational and behavioral — a kind of digital mindfulness: training teams on cognitive biases, persuasion techniques, and disinformation. And silos give way to ecosystems that integrate IT, operational technology, and physical security, since everything is interconnected. The takeaway is clear: security becomes a matter of cognitive literacy, where the real firewall is a trained mind.

Notice how everything fits together. Needs move a person; the SRAA decides what they perceive; addiction hijacks reward; Shiny Object Syndrome and distraction scatter focus. In the end, security itself depends on trained attention. Understanding the organ at stake is no academic curiosity — it is the prerequisite for everything in the next learning paths.
