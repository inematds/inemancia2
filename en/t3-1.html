Section 1 of 2·Motivation vs. determination

1.Motivation vs. determination

The biggest mistake people who don't make progress almost never make is a lack of willpower — it's the kind of fuel they rely on. 1Motivation comes from outside: the inspiring video, the catchy quote, someone else's opinion. It's like a rocket without fuel: it rises quickly and soon falls, because everything that comes from outside is temporary. When the glow of inspiration fades, the task remains untouched — and there's no drive to do it.

A determination and is of a different nature. It doesn’t come from other people’s words; it comes from within you, built on a clear purpose, solid goals, and a plan of action. It doesn’t depend on applause or mood. It grows the moment you truly decide that you’re going to make it happen—and stay the course when no one is watching, on a rainy day, when you’re tired, before any results appear.

Two different but complementary concepts

It’s not about choosing one and discarding the other. Motivation is the initial impulse: the “why” that gets you started — fluctuating, emotional, dependent on internal and external factors. Determination is the continuity: commitment to the goal despite obstacles — steady, rational, built on discipline and habit. Motivation is deciding to run a marathon; determination is training every day, even in the rain.2

Stop and predict

You started a course excited and stopped in the third week. The enthusiasm was real — so what kept you from finishing?

See a possible answer

Missing determination, not motivation. Enthusiasm did its job—it got you started. But it’s a flash in the pan: it flares up quickly and dies out at the first difficulty. What gets you to the finish is doing it even when you don’t feel like it, sustained by discipline, clear goals, and a plan for when your motivation runs out.

There’s a subtle trap here: for many people, motivation works like self-deception. Watching something inspiring releases dopamine and gives you a sense of progress—even when you haven’t done anything concrete. It’s an illusory satisfaction that paralyzes action, because it’s more comfortable to seek the next inspiration than to face the hard work. True satisfaction comes from doing, not feeling.

▸ Going deeper: the four pillars of determination optional

The happy path above is enough to use the idea. This layer explains what sustains determination when motivation is absent — and can be skipped without missing anything.

Strong determination usually rests on four pillars. Clarity of purpose: honestly knowing why you're doing this. Discipline: the ability to keep going on difficult days. Commitment: doing what needs to be done, regardless of the circumstances. And resilience: the strength to continue in the face of failure. Notice that neither depends on being inspired — they are structures, not moods.

That’s why the practice is: cultivate motivation to renew your enthusiasm (visualize the purpose, celebrate small gains) and strengthen determination to sustain the work (create routines, focus on minimal progress). One sparks it; the other keeps it burning.

Section 2 of 2·Permission

2.Permission to succeed

You may have ability — knowledge and skill — and disposition — energy and effort — and still not making progress. A third piece is missing, almost always invisible: permission.3 Permission is the level of internal and external acceptance you have to achieve a result. It’s what you believe you can have, achieve, or experience without guilt, fear, or self-sabotage.

Without permission, you’ll always find a way back to your “comfort zone.” If, deep down, earning more money feels dangerous, or like it could distance you from your family, or turn you into “a different person,” you’ll sabotage yourself— turning down the promotion, charging less than you’re worth, spending it all as soon as your income grows. Not because you lack ability, but because your brain hasn’t accepted the new reality yet. That’s why extremely qualified people sometimes earn less than people with half their qualifications.

Where the block is hiding

Permission isn’t just about money: it touches every area. The professional who doesn’t apply for a promotion “because they’re not ready yet.” The person who sabotages a healthy relationship because “it’s too good to be true.” Someone who associates rest with laziness and never takes a vacation. The young person who hides their calling for fear of disapproval. In every case, the wording changes, but the root is the same: an invisible limit on what you accept for yourself.4

Stop and predict

Someone really wants to move forward, knows exactly what to do, but doesn’t act — and ends up giving up. How can you tell whether the problem is a lack of permission or just a lack of priority?

See a possible answer

By the emotion when giving up. If, when stopping, the person feels relief, it was a conscious choice—she simply didn’t want to pay the price. If you feel frustration and regret, it was a lack of permission: she wanted to, but an internal block stopped her from allowing herself to move forward.

▸ Going deeper: wanting the top, refusing the path optional

Optional layer. Not every block is permission — it’s important not to confuse the two.

Some people want the top of the mountain but don’t want to face the climb: fascinated by success, resistant to effort. They look only at the result achieved by those who made it and ignore the years of the journey that came before — comparing behind-the-scenes with the final stage, seeking shortcuts that don’t exist, and losing heart when the reality of the process sets in. Here, the obstacle isn’t permission: it’s the illusion that an easy path exists.

The practical solution is the same in every case: accept that every success has a price, stop comparing your beginning with someone else's peak, adjust your expectations, and focus on first step — because those who only look at the summit get intimidated and give up before they start. And when the barrier is truly about permission, it can be expanded: the first step is recognizing where the invisible limits are.

Motivation as self-deception: watching something inspiring releases dopamine and creates a sense of progress without anything concrete being done — an illusory satisfaction that paralyzes real action.
Source
The distinction between impulse and consistency connects with Unlimited Power, by Anthony Robbins, which treats emotional control, focus, and positive habits as trainable strategies—not luck.
Definition
Permission. The level of internal and external acceptance needed to achieve a result without guilt, fear, or self-sabotage. The success equation is ability + willingness + permission — without the last one, effort goes to waste.
Going deeper
Four patterns that block prosperity: the natural victim, the intentional victim, the avenger (who thinks money corrupts), and the narcissist. Recognizing them is the first step toward expanding your sense of permission.
